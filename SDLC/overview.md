# SDLC Overview

The **Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC)** provides a structured way to organize how software is planned, designed, developed, tested, delivered and maintained. Understanding SDLC helps explain how development activities are connected, how quality is addressed throughout the lifecycle and why different projects may use different development models.

The **SDLC** module develops from understanding the basic lifecycle and sequential development models to understanding iterative, incremental and Agile approaches. This progression shows how different models organize development, testing, feedback and change.

![SDLC overview](./assets/images/sdlc_overview.png)

**Level 1** introduces the foundations of SDLC. It explains **what SDLC is**, **why SDLC exists**, the **main phases of SDLC**, and the importance of **phase deliverables and completion criteria**. It also introduces the role of **static and dynamic testing** throughout the lifecycle and examines two sequential SDLC models: the **Waterfall model** and the **V-Model**. The level concludes by comparing how these models organize development and testing, including the V-Model's explicit relationship between development activities and corresponding test levels.

**Level 2** focuses on development approaches that support repeated cycles, gradual delivery and adaptation. It explains the difference between **iterative development** and **incremental development**, how these ideas can be combined, and how testing and feedback can occur throughout development cycles. It then introduces **Agile**, the values of the **Agile Manifesto**, the **whole team approach** and examples of Agile approaches such as **Scrum, Kanban and Extreme Programming (XP)**. The level concludes by comparing **sequential and iterative approaches**, including their different ways of handling feedback, change, testing and project trade-offs.

Together, these levels establish a foundation for understanding that SDLC is not a single fixed method. Different SDLC models organize the same broad development concerns in different ways, and the appropriate approach depends on factors such as requirement stability, project risk, stakeholder involvement, the need for feedback and the amount of expected change.
